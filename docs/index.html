How the lid traps smog

Sketch, not to scale. Heights from ERA5 for 11 to 19 June 2026.

Santiago lies in a basin about 500 m above sea level, between the Coastal Range to the west and the Andes to the east. In winter the air in the basin is often calm.

On clear winter nights the ground cools quickly and the air touching it becomes colder than the air above. This temperature inversion works like a lid: cold, heavy air near the ground cannot rise through the warmer air on top, so smoke from traffic, industry and wood stoves stays in a shallow layer.

The lid height on this page is ERA5's boundary layer height, the depth of air that mixes freely. During this episode it fell to 13 to 30 m every night, so a whole night of emissions was packed into very little air. In the afternoon the sun warms the ground and the layer grows, diluting the smog. On 12 June it reached only about 140 m, and PM2.5 climbed for the next two days. Smog lifted during the day stays aloft at night in the residual layer (drawn here as an illustration).

PM2.5 was highest in the west of the city (Pudahuel and Cerro Navia, 132 µg/m³ on 14 June) and lowest at Las Condes, higher up towards the Andes, which stayed between 21 and 33 until 17 June.

Episodes are declared a day ahead from a forecast. Measured as a 24-hour average, 80 to 109 µg/m³ is the alerta range and 110 to 169 the preemergencia range (dashed lines in the chart). The episode ended when the lid lifted: on 18 June the afternoon mixing layer reached about 685 m and PM2.5 at Cerro Navia fell below 60.
